Field guide

Clues worth noticing

Use these when a specimen lands between two possible readings.

Bottle lips

Finish style and seam position can narrow manufacturing period.

Pontil scars

A rough or polished base scar can signal older hand-finished production.

Embossing

Letters, numbers, and logos can point to a maker or regional bottler.

Bubbles

Irregular bubbles can support an older-manufacture hypothesis, but are not proof alone.

Opacity

Opaque white may indicate milk glass rather than frosted clear glass.

Near-black glass

Hold it to strong light; deep olive or amber can look black at thickness.

After storms

Spring, fall, and strong wave events can reshape the shoreline and expose new glass.

Pebble lines

Search where small stones gather near the waterline; glass often settles with material of similar size.

Color in daylight

Thickness, lighting, weathering, and glass chemistry can all shift how a color appears.

Glassy rock?

Leland Blue and similar material are industrial smelting slag, not ordinary bottle glass.

About the index

How Lakeglass reads a find

A field identification is an interpretation of physical clues, not a laboratory attribution.

Identification

Lakeglass compares color, opacity, thickness, form, surface wear, markings, and shoreline context. When a diagnostic feature such as a rim, base, embossing, near-black color, milk glass, or slag candidate is reported, the guide asks one additional targeted question because those clues can carry more historical weight than color alone.

Identification strength

Possible · Good match · Strong match ÷ Distinctive match. These labels describe how well the observed clues agree; they are not statistical probabilities.

Occurrence index

The 1–10 score is a comparative Lakeglass field index for how often a color is encountered around Lake Michigan. It is not a measured population frequency and can change as better regional evidence becomes available.

Provenance

Where and when you found a specimen are recorded as known provenance. Probable source, age, and historical origin remain inferred interpretations and may change when better evidence appears.

Limits

A fragment usually cannot be dated or assigned to a specific manufacturer from color alone. Embossing, bottle finishes, bases, mold seams, and other diagnostic features can substantially strengthen a reading.

Research sources

Lakeglass cross-checks its field notes against Beachcombing Magazine, Rock & Gem, Schlitz Audubon Nature Center, Visit Southwest Michigan, Michigan Beachtowns, and regional Michigan reporting. Regional observations are used as context rather than treated as universal rules, and source-backed information is rewritten into concise field-guide language.
