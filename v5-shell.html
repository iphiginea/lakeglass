Field guide

Clues worth noticing

The best dates usually come from manufacturing evidence rather than generic shard shape.

Pontil scars

On utilitarian bottles, a pontil usually points to the Civil War era or earlier and is almost always pre-1870s.

Applied vs. tooled finish

Applied finishes typically date about 1820–1890. Tooled finishes dominate after 1890 and largely disappear by the early 1920s.

Owens suction scar

A feathered circular or oval base scar identifies the Owens automatic-bottle process beginning in the early 20th century.

Vent marks

Mouth-blown bottles with air-vent dots generally date after the mid/late 1880s; multiple vents often favor roughly 1905–1920.

Embossing

Letters, numbers, logos, and date codes can identify a maker, product, plant, or narrower manufacturing period.

Solarized lavender

A pale or uneven amethyst cast can support manganese-decolorized colorless glass, commonly around 1890–1920.

Clear-glass tint

Faint amethyst, straw, or gray in otherwise clear glass can carry more dating value than “clear” alone.

Near-black glass

Strong backlight can reveal deep olive, amber, blue, or purple and change the historical interpretation.

True flat glass

A small flat shard is not automatically window glass. Deliberate rippling or other flat-glass manufacturing clues carry more weight.

Industrial slag

Known furnace context can be highly dateable: Leland and Frankfort/Elberta slag can tie to specific ironworks periods.

About the index

How Lakeglass reads a find

Each accession contains two layers: observations recorded from the object and an interpretation that can change as better evidence becomes available.

Observation vs. interpretation

Color, thickness, surviving form, opacity, surface, marks, diagnostic features, and known find provenance are stored as observations. Probable source, age, dating confidence, and historical significance are interpretations.

Dating confidence

Broad means the evidence spans a long period. Moderate means several clues favor a useful range. Strong means a documented color chemistry or manufacturing feature narrows the range substantially. Diagnostic is reserved for especially powerful clues such as a pontil, distinctive finish/manufacturing feature, or location-specific industrial attribution.

Three 1–10 scales

Color rarity: 1 very common → 10 exceptionally rare. Form distinctiveness: 1 generic fragment → 10 unusually diagnostic form. Historical interest: 1 little chronological information → 10 unusually informative.

Re-analysis

An accession number and known provenance stay permanent. The current interpretation may be re-run when Lakeglass research changes; earlier interpretations remain in the record rather than being erased.

Research sources

Lakeglass uses the Society for Historical Archaeology Historic Glass Bottle Identification resources alongside regional Lake Michigan sources including Schlitz Audubon, Southwest Michigan reporting, Rock & Gem, Michigan Beachtowns, and Beachcombing Magazine. Regional observations are context, not universal rules.
